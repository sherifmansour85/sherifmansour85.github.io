12 questions to ask before choosing a clinic abroad — and what a good answer sounds like

Most bad medical travel experiences are not caused by bad surgeons. They are caused by questions nobody asked. This is the list we would want a member of our own family to use — including the awkward ones, and what a good answer actually sounds like. Use it on us exactly as you would on anyone else.

1. Who specifically will perform my procedure?

You want a name, a specialty, and their specific training in the procedure you need — not "our team of experts".

A good answer names the doctor and describes their qualifications and exact specialty. A weak answer keeps the surgeon anonymous until you arrive. If you cannot find out who will operate on you before you travel, that is information in itself.

2. Is this doctor licensed, and can I verify it?

Licensing is verifiable in most countries. A provider should be willing to give you the doctor’s registration details so you can check independently.

Be wary of vague claims of international training without specifics. "Trained in Europe" is not a qualification; a named certificate from a named institution is.

3. Where will the procedure actually take place?

The clinic where you consult is not always the facility where you are treated. Ask for the name and address of the actual operating location, and whether it is licensed for that procedure.

For surgery under general anaesthesia, ask specifically about on-site emergency and intensive care capability.

4. Is the facility accredited, and by whom?

Accreditation claims should always come with a body, a scope and a current status. "Internationally accredited" on its own means nothing.

If a provider cannot name the accrediting body, treat the claim as undocumented. We apply this to ourselves: we will not publish an accreditation for a partner facility without documentation.

5. What exactly does the quoted price include?

Ask for it itemised: doctor’s fees, facility fees, anaesthesia, tests, implants or materials, medication, follow-up appointments, and accommodation and transfers if they are part of the package.

Then ask the most revealing question: what does it not include?

6. What could add to the cost, and how likely is that?

Common additions include bone grafting in dental work, a second session in hair transplant, revision procedures, extended hospital stay, and treatment of complications.

A provider who raises these before you ask is showing you how they operate. One who says there are never any additional costs is either inexperienced or not being straight with you.

7. Is this a final price or an estimate?

For almost any procedure requiring clinical assessment, the honest answer before examination is "an estimate".

A fixed final price from photographs alone — especially for dental implants or any surgery — is a warning sign, not a service. One Cairo dental clinic states in its patient guide that no serious implant clinic should quote finally before assessing bone volume by imaging. That is the correct standard.

8. What happens if a complication occurs?

Ask specifically: who is responsible, what does it cost, and what happens if it appears after I return home?

You want to know whether corrective treatment is covered, who to call outside working hours, and how coordination with a doctor in your own country would work. The answer matters more than the likelihood.

9. How long will I actually need to stay?

Not how long the procedure takes — how long until I am cleared to fly. Those are very different numbers.

Anyone offering you surgery with a three-day total trip is not planning for your recovery. Flying too soon after an operation carries real clot risk.

10. What follow-up happens after I return home?

Ask what happens in the weeks after you fly. Is there a scheduled review? Will you get a written summary of what was done, a medication list and instructions? Can you reach the treating doctor with a question?

A provider whose involvement ends at the airport is offering you a transaction, not care.

11. How is my medical information handled?

You will be sending scans, reports and possibly photographs. Ask who receives them, how they are stored, how long they are kept, and whether they are shared outside the treating provider.

You are entitled to a clear answer, and entitled to refuse the use of your case or images in marketing.

12. What is your relationship with the clinic you recommend?

This is the question people most hesitate to ask a coordinator, and the most reasonable one to ask.

Ask whether the recommendation is based on your clinical need or a commercial arrangement, and whether alternatives were considered. You are entitled to know how a provider was chosen for you.
