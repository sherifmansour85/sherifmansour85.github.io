12 Questions to Ask Before Choosing a Clinic Abroad — and the Answers You Should Expect

Most bad medical travel experiences are not caused by bad surgeons. They are caused by questions nobody asked. This is the checklist we would want a member of our own family to use — including the questions that are uncomfortable to ask, and what a good answer actually sounds like. Use it on us as much as on anyone else.

1. Who exactly will perform my procedure?

You want a name, a specialty, and their specific training in the procedure you need — not "our team of experts".

A good answer names the physician and describes their qualifications and subspecialty. A poor answer keeps the surgeon anonymous until you arrive. If you cannot find out who is operating on you before you fly, that is information in itself.

2. Is that physician licensed, and can I verify it?

Licensing is verifiable in most countries. A provider should be willing to give you the physician's registration details so you can check independently.

Be wary of vague claims of international training with no specifics. "Trained in Europe" is not a credential; a named qualification from a named institution is.

3. Where will the procedure actually take place?

The clinic you consult at is not always the facility where you are treated. Ask for the name and address of the actual operating location, and whether it is licensed for the procedure.

For surgery under general anaesthesia, ask specifically about emergency and intensive care capability on site.

4. Is the facility accredited, and by whom?

Accreditation claims should always come with a body, a scope and a current status. "Internationally accredited" on its own means nothing.

If a provider cannot name the accrediting organisation, treat the claim as unverified. We apply this to ourselves: we will not publish an accreditation for a partner facility without documentation.

5. What exactly is included in the quoted price?

Ask for it itemised: physician fees, facility fees, anaesthesia, investigations, implants or materials, medication, follow-up appointments, and accommodation and transfers if they are part of the package.

Then ask the more revealing question: what is NOT included?

6. What could add to the cost, and how likely is that?

Common additions include bone grafting in dental work, a second session in hair restoution, revision procedures, extended hospital stay, and treatment of complications.

A provider who volunteers these before you ask is showing you how they operate. One who says there are never additional costs is either inexperienced or not telling you the truth.

7. Is this a final price or an estimate?

For almost any procedure requiring clinical assessment, the honest answer before examination is "estimate".

A firm final price quoted from photographs alone — particularly for dental implants or any surgery — is a warning sign, not a service. One Cairo dental clinic states in its own guide that no reputable implant clinic should quote a firm price before examining bone volume via imaging. That is the correct standard.

8. What happens if there is a complication?

Ask specifically: who is responsible, what does it cost, and what happens if it appears after I have flown home?

You want to know whether revision or corrective treatment is covered, who you contact out of hours, and how coordination with a doctor in your own country would work. The answer matters more than the likelihood.

9. How long will I actually need to stay?

Not how long the procedure takes — how long until you are cleared to fly. These are very different numbers.

Anyone offering surgery with a three-day total trip is not planning your recovery. Flying too soon after an operation carries a genuine risk of blood clots.

10. What follow-up is there after I return home?

Ask what happens in the weeks after you leave. Is there a scheduled review? Will you receive a written summary of what was done, a medication list and instructions? Can you reach the treating physician with a question?

A provider whose involvement ends at the airport is offering you a transaction, not care.

11. How is my medical information handled?

You will be sending scans, reports and sometimes photographs. Ask who receives them, how they are stored, how long they are kept, and whether they are shared beyond the treating provider.

You are entitled to a clear answer and to say no to marketing use of your case or images.

12. What is your relationship to the clinic you are recommending?

This is the question people are most reluctant to ask a coordinator, and the most reasonable one.

Ask whether the recommendation is based on your clinical need or on a commercial arrangement, and whether alternatives were considered. You are entitled to know how a provider was chosen for you.

Our own position: we coordinate with independent licensed providers, we verify licensing before listing anyone, and we will tell you why a particular provider suits your case. We do not deliver treatment and we do not guarantee clinical outcomes.
